A data-driven management accounting case study diagnosing why higher sales failed to translate into profit across three Singapore café outlets—and turning transaction analysis into differentiated commercial and operational decisions.

In brief

What the analysis found

01

Growth masked a profitability problem. Total sales rose to approximately $3.95 million, while profit fell to $544,056. Direct costs grew 21.19%—far faster than revenue.

02

Each location had a different economic problem. Orchard's sales mix weakened contribution; CBD's capacity and marketing spend were poorly aligned with demand; Toa Payoh grew profit and served as a comparative benchmark.

03

Decisions should be contribution-led, not sales-led. Pricing, menu mix, staffing, marketing and opening hours require outlet-specific, relevant-cost evaluations and pilot testing before implementation.

01 · Business problem

Why were growing sales producing lower profits?

Merlion Café's three outlets—CBD, Orchard and Toa Payoh—served different demand patterns under largely uniform pricing, menu, staffing and opening-hour practices. The team examined 2025–2026 financial and transactional case data to isolate the drivers of changing profitability and assess practical management responses.

Decision question: Which outlet-specific changes to product mix, pricing, marketing and capacity could improve incremental contribution without assuming that higher revenue automatically creates value?
02 · Analytical framework

From raw transaction data to decisions

The group combined Python-based transaction reconstruction with management accounting, operating analysis and scenario evaluation.

STEP 01

Reconcile the dataset

Rebuild product-level revenue and direct costs from transaction quantities, prices and cost schedules; reconcile totals to case statements within rounding differences.

STEP 02

Decompose profit changes

Construct outlet profit bridges separating volume, product mix, selling-price, direct-cost-rate and operating-expense effects.

STEP 03

Analyse service economics

Compare contribution per order, service channel, modelled fulfilment minute, staff capacity, weekday and hourly demand.

STEP 04

Evaluate decisions

Stress-test selective price changes, marketing break-even orders and opening hours using incremental contribution and avoidable costs.

STEP 05

Translate into management KPIs

Propose a strategy map and Balanced Scorecard centred on contribution quality, cost control, demand fit and operational efficiency.

Method assumptions included an order key of outlet + date + order ID, and modelled fulfilment time of 3 minutes plus 2 minutes per dine-in item (40% of that time for takeaway/delivery). These are case-model assumptions rather than observed time-and-motion measurements.

04 · Key findings

Diagnose the economics, not just the symptoms

Orchard · Product mix

Profit fell 32.67% despite higher sales. A −$241,740 pure product-mix effect dominated its profit bridge, reflecting a shift away from several high-contribution products.

CBD · Capacity and marketing

Modelled fulfilment utilisation was just 40.85%. Marketing spend rose by $16,800 while annual orders declined by 425; weekends contributed only 4.18% of revenue.

Toa Payoh · Internal benchmark

Sales grew 8.55% and profit increased 9.79%. Its $17.02 contribution per order was higher than Orchard's $14.70 despite fewer orders.

Chain-wide · Direct-cost pressure

Direct costs rose 21.19%, against 5.66% sales growth. Gross margin declined from 58.69% to 53.03%, supporting tighter cost governance.

Service-channel modelling also showed higher direct contribution per modelled fulfilment minute for takeaway and delivery than dine-in. This is an operational workload signal—not proof of higher net channel profitability, since delivery commissions were unavailable.

05 · Management decisions

Move from diagnosis to quantified actions

Orchard
Menu & pricing

Rebuild higher-contribution product share through placement and bundling, then test selective price changes. A $0.50 increase under an assumed 5% volume decline produced an estimated +$54,956 in annual direct contribution in the case sensitivity model—not a realised outcome.

CBD
Marketing & capacity

Align shifts with weekday office peaks and require contribution-based campaign approval. The extra $16,800 in annual marketing spend required around 1,667 incremental orders to break even at the reported contribution per order.

Opening hours
Relevant costs

Do not assume closure saves labour. Under the case's stated assumptions, a full CBD weekend closure generated an estimated −$10,951 net effect before labour savings; a shorter Orchard hours pilot showed a positive but assumption-dependent result.

Chain-wide
Performance control

Monitor contribution per order and hour, direct-cost percentage, modelled utilisation and product mix in a Balanced Scorecard. At approximately $3.95 million in sales, a one-percentage-point lower direct-cost ratio would imply about +$39,500 gross profit, all else equal.

Recommended operating principle: centralise direct-cost governance, but adapt pricing, menu mix, marketing, staffing and opening hours to each outlet's local demand and contribution economics.
06 · Assumptions & limitations

Where the evidence stops

  • Simulated case setting: financial figures and proposed actions come from the academic Merlion Café case. Recommendations were not implemented, and modelled benefits are not actual savings.
  • Capacity measure: 40.85% at CBD measures modelled order-fulfilment workload relative to assumed capacity, not all employee activity or labour productivity.
  • Customer and channel data: the dataset lacked customer identifiers and delivery-platform commission costs, limiting conclusions about repeat customers or complete channel profitability.
  • Scenario uncertainty: price responses, avoidable labour costs, customer migration between hours and several cost behaviours were not observed. Pilot testing was therefore recommended before operational rollout.
07 · Recruiter takeaway

What this project demonstrates

This group project demonstrates how quantitative work can support practical financial decisions: reconciling raw data, decomposing earnings drivers, distinguishing volume growth from contribution quality, assessing operational constraints, evaluating relevant-cost scenarios and presenting defensible recommendations to management.

The full group report includes reconciliation schedules, profit bridges, product-mix analysis, capacity and service-channel metrics, demand heatmaps, pricing sensitivity tables, and the strategy map/Balanced Scorecard.
