01 · Investment question

How should risk and return influence portfolio allocation?

The project examines how two U.S. equities with different risk-return characteristics can be combined to construct more efficient portfolios.

Berkshire Hathaway (BRK-B) and AT&T (T) were selected for the portfolio analysis. Their historical returns, volatility, covariance and correlation were used to evaluate different portfolio weight combinations.

A second analysis extends the study to 30 U.S. equities by estimating each stock's sensitivity to S&P 500 market returns.

Core question: How can portfolio weights be adjusted to minimise risk or improve risk-adjusted return, and how sensitive are individual stocks to broader market movements?
02 · Data

Daily equity and market data across a three-month sample.

The study uses daily closing-price data from 2 January to 1 April 2025.

The 61 closing-price observations were converted into 60 daily holding-period returns for portfolio and regression analysis.

06 · Diversification

Positive correlation limits diversification benefits.

BRK-B and AT&T produced an estimated correlation coefficient of approximately 0.461.

The moderate positive relationship means the two assets do not move perfectly together, but they also do not provide the same diversification benefits that could be achieved using assets with lower or negative correlation.

Portfolio implication: Diversification is driven not only by the risk of individual assets, but also by how their returns move relative to one another.

08 · Limitations

The analysis demonstrates portfolio mechanics rather than producing investment recommendations.

The study covers only 60 daily return observations across approximately three months. This is a short estimation window for expected returns, volatility, correlation and beta.

The Markowitz analysis also considers only two risky assets, meaning the efficient frontier is useful for illustrating the portfolio optimisation process but is not representative of a broadly diversified institutional portfolio.

Historical averages and covariance are used as model inputs. These estimates may be unstable and may not represent future market conditions.

A 0.2% risk-free rate was assumed for the Capital Allocation Line analysis, while dividends were excluded from the daily holding-period return calculations.

Finally, most of the estimated 30-stock betas were statistically insignificant, so these estimates should not be interpreted as robust long-term measures of systematic risk.
