The Impact of Exchange Rate Volatility on Export Growth

A quantitative time-series study examining how MYR/USD volatility relates to Malaysia's export growth in the short and long run, using a two-stage volatility and dynamic-regression framework.

In brief

What the analysis found

01

Persistent currency volatility matters. MYR/USD volatility was associated with weaker Malaysian export growth in the long run, while the short-run effect was not statistically significant.

02

The relationship is dynamic. The volatility-export relationship shifted from positive in 2006–2015 to negative in 2016–2025, suggesting sensitivity to changing macroeconomic conditions.

03

Global demand matters more in the short run. World industrial production and domestic inflation carried stronger short-run predictive signals than exchange-rate volatility itself.

01 · Research question

Does currency volatility materially affect export growth?

Malaysia is a highly trade-oriented economy operating under a floating exchange-rate regime. The project investigates whether volatility in MYR/USD affects export performance, whether the effect differs between the short and long run, and whether volatility contains predictive information for exports.

Core objective: distinguish temporary currency noise from persistent exchange-rate risk that may influence exporters' pricing, hedging and long-term decisions.
02 · Methodology

A two-stage modelling framework

The study combines high-frequency FX information with monthly macroeconomic data, then tests both volatility dynamics and export-growth relationships.

STEP 01

Prepare data

MYR/USD exchange rates plus export growth, REER, industrial production, CPI and world industrial production.

STEP 02

Estimate volatility

Model daily MYR/USD log returns using ARIMA and GARCH-family specifications; aggregate conditional volatility to monthly frequency.

STEP 03

Estimate dynamics

Apply ARDL bounds testing and an Error Correction Model to separate long-run cointegration from short-run adjustment.

STEP 04

Stress-test results

Use stationarity, residual diagnostics, subsample stability, CUSUM/CUSUMSQ and Granger-causality testing.

03 · Data

Macroeconomic controls around the FX signal

  • Exchange rate: daily MYR/USD observations used to estimate conditional volatility.
  • Export growth: Malaysia's year-on-year export growth.
  • Controls: Real Effective Exchange Rate, Industrial Production Index, Consumer Price Index and World Industrial Production Index.
  • Study horizon: January 2006 to September 2025, covering major global shocks and changing monetary conditions.
04 · Key findings

Persistent volatility matters more than short-term noise.

Negative long-run relationship

Exchange-rate volatility carried a negative long-run coefficient and was statistically significant at the 10% level.

No significant short-run effect

The short-run volatility coefficient was negative but statistically insignificant, suggesting immediate export movements were driven more by other demand- and supply-side factors.

Adjustment back to equilibrium

The error-correction coefficient implied that about 29.2% of a short-run deviation from long-run equilibrium was corrected within one month.

Regime dependence

Subsample analysis showed the volatility-export relationship shifting from positive in 2006–2015 to negative in 2016–2025.

05 · Interpretation

What the results suggest

The evidence supports a distinction between transitory FX movements and persistent currency uncertainty. In the short run, exporters may partly absorb or hedge exchange-rate shocks, while domestic production conditions and global demand have greater influence on month-to-month export performance. Over longer horizons, persistent volatility may increase uncertainty and reinforce risk-averse behaviour.

Granger-causality results also suggest that exchange-rate volatility is a secondary predictor of export growth, while world industrial production and CPI carry stronger predictive signals in the model.

06 · Robustness & limitations

Testing the stability of the conclusion

  • CUSUM results supported broad coefficient stability, while CUSUMSQ highlighted variance instability around periods of major economic stress.
  • The study identifies multicollinearity among macroeconomic controls as a limitation affecting individual coefficient precision.
  • Aggregate export data may mask sector-level differences.
  • A linear ARDL framework may not capture all asymmetric or nonlinear effects, motivating potential NARDL or sectoral extensions.
07 · Recruiter takeaway

What this project demonstrates

This project demonstrates the ability to translate an open-ended macro-financial question into a testable modelling pipeline, implement time-series techniques in Python, evaluate model assumptions, interpret statistical evidence cautiously and communicate findings in a decision-oriented format.
